# Do Water User Associations Work? Evidence from Minor Irrigation Tanks in Vidarbha

## Abstract

Maharashtra handed over the management of many minor irrigation tanks to water user associations (WUAs) after the Maharashtra Management of Irrigation Systems by Farmers Act, 2005. This paper asks whether the handover changed how water is shared, how canals are maintained and how much farmers at the tail end of the canal receive. We study 48 tanks in Yavatmal and Washim districts using irrigation department records from 2012 to 2023 and a survey of 1,140 farm households conducted between 6 November 2023 and 22 December 2023. Tail-end farmers in villages with an active WUA received water on 3.2 more days per rabi season than those in villages where the association existed only on paper. The difference is largest where the WUA collected water charges regularly and spent them on desilting the canals.

## 1. Introduction

Across India, irrigation reforms since the 1990s have tried to move the management of canals from government engineers to farmers themselves. The argument is simple. Farmers know their fields, their neighbours and their canals better than any department, and if they control the water, they will maintain teh system and share it more fairly. In practice, many associations were formed in a hurry to meet targets, and studies from Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat and Maharashtra report that a large share exists only on paper (Mollinga et al., 2004; Shah, 2009).

In Vidarbha, minor irrigation tanks are the the main source of protective irrigation for the rabi crop. A typical tank irrigates between 200 and 600 hectares through a main canal and a network of field channels and nullahs. When the canals silt up or the gates are damaged, water stops long before it reaches the tail, and the farmers there go back to growing a single rainfed crop of cotton or soybean.

Our study was led by Dr. Prashant Wankhede of the Rashtrasant Tukadoji Maharaj Nagpur University, with fieldwork coordinated by Ms. Sneha Deshmukh. Prof. Anil Thakre advised on the hydrology. In each village we met the sarpanch, the secretary of the WUA where one existed, the talathi and at least two groups of farmers, one near the head of the canal and one near the tail.

## 2. How the Associations Are Supposed to Work

Under the Act, a WUA is registered for each canal command, and the irrigation department hands over the operation of the canal below a fixed point. The association receives water in bulk,  decides the rotation schedule, collects water charges from members and keeps a share of the charges for maintenance. In theory, the gram panchayat has no formal role, but in most of our villages the sarpanch was either the WUA president or closely connected to him.

Each association is supposed to hold a general body meeting before every season, prepare a rotation schedule and keep accounts. The department, in turn, must release water on agreed dates and repair the main structures. Much depends on whether both sides keep there part of the bargain.

## 3. Data

We combined three sources. First, the irrigation department's recods of water releases, area irrigated and water charges for each tank. Second, our household survey, which recorded the crops grown, the number of irrigations received and the timing of each one. Third, a short assessment of each association, based on its registers, meeting minutes and bank passbook.

Using the assessment, we classed associations as active if they had held at least one general body meeting in each of the previous two years, collected water charges in at least one of those years and spent money on canal maintenance. By this measure, 19 of the 48 tanks had an active WUA, 17 had an association that existed but was inactive, and 12 had no association at all.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Water at the tail

Farmers at the tail of the canal in villages with an active WUA received an average of 6.8 days of water per rabi season, compared with 3.6 days in villages with an inactive association and 3.1 days where no association existed. Head-end farmers received roughly the same amount in all three groups, which suggests that the gains at the tail did did not come at the cost of farmers near the head.

The difference shows up in cropping. In active-WUA villages, 41% of tail-end households grew a rabi crop of wheat or gram, against 19% elsewhere. Several farmers told us that the reliable second watering was the difference between a crop and a loss. One farmer in Washim said, "Before, the water came till the culvert and stopped. Now it comes till my field, at least twice."

### 4.2 Maintenance and money

Active associations spent an average of ₹1.8 lakh per year on maintenance, mostly on desilting the main canal and repairing field channels before the rabi season. The money came from water charges, which active WUAs collected at about ₹400 per acre per season, and from occasional grants under the state's tank rehabilitation scheme. Inactive associations spent almost nothing, and in several cases the department did the desilting itself, usually late.

Collection rates mattered more then the charge itself. Associations that collected from more than 70% of members were able to plan maintenance in advance, while those that collected from fewer than half was always short of money and depended on the department.

### 4.3 What makes an association active?

We looked at the characteristics of active and inactive associations. Three factors stands out. Active associations were more likely to have a secretary who was paid a small honorarium, usually ₹1,500 a month, and who kept the registers up to date. They were more likely to include tail-end farmers on the managing committee. And they were less likely to be dominated by a single large landowner. Caste and landholding patterns in the village also mattered, but less than we expected once these three factors were taken into account.

The role of the sarpanch was mixed. In some villages, a sarpanch who also led the WUA was able to mobilise labour for desilting through shramdaan and to bring in funds from other schemes. In others, the association became a extension of panchayat politics, and tail-end farmers from the opposing faction stopped paying charges because they did not trust how the money was spent.

## 5. Discussion

Our results suggest that water user associations can make a real difference, but only when they function as associations rather than as registrations. The gains for tail-end farmers are substantial and come without obvious losses at the head. At the same time, more than half of the tanks in our sample had an association that was inactive or non-existant, which means the potential benefits are largely unrealised.

Three practical lessons follow. First, a paid secretary appears to be worth far more than the honorarium costs. Second, including tail-end farmers on the committee is not just fair but effective, because they are the ones with the strongest reason to keep the canal clean. Third, the irrigation department's own performance matters: associations lose interest quickly when releases are late or repairs to the main structures are not done.

There are limits to what our data can show. Associations were not assigned at random, and active WUAs may have formed in villages that were already better organised. We tried to account for this by comparing villages within the same block and controlling for land distribution, literacy and distance to the taluka town, but we cannot rule out other differences. The survey also relies on farmers' recall of the number of irrigations, which effects precision.

## 6. Conclusion

In Vidarbha's minor irrigation tanks, an active water user association roughly doubled the water reaching farmers at the tail of the canal. The associations that worked collected charges regularly, paid a secretary, included tail-end farmers and spent their money on desilting before the rabi season. Most associations did none of these these things. Making the existing law work, rather than writing new ones, may be the most useful step for irrigation policy in the region.
