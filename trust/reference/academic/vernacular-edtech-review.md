# Learning in the Mother Tongue: A Review of Vernacular EdTech in Indian Schools

## Introduction

Over the past decade, educational technology in India has moved from a niche market to a mass one. Smartphone ownership has spread to most rural households, data prices have fallen sharply, and the school closures of 2020 and 2021 pushed millions of families to try learning apps for the first time. Yet most of the content on these platforms was designed in English, for children in private urban schools. This review asks what we know about digital learning tools that teach in Indian languages, and whether they help children in government schools learn more.

The question matter because language is one of the strongest predictors of learning in Indian classrooms. The National Education Policy 2020 recommends that children be taught in their home language at least until Class 5, wherever possible. The Annual Status of Education Report 2022 found that only 20.5% of children in Class 3 could read a Class 2 text (ASER Centre, 2023). Many of these children are taught in a language they do not speak at home. If technology is to help close this gap, it must work in the languages children actually use.

We reviewed studies published between January 2015 and June 2024 that evaluated a digital learning tool delivered in at least one Indian language other than English. We searched Google Scholar, ERIC and the repositories of major research organisations, and we contacted researchers at J-PAL South Asia and the Azim Premji University for unpublished work. After screening, 27 studies met our criteria. The review was conducted by Dr. Ananya Bose and Prof. Mohan Reddy, with help from two research assistants.

## What kinds of tools have been studied?

The studies fall into three broad groups. The first group covers adaptive learning software used in school computer labs or after-school centres. The best known example is Mindspark, which was evaluated in Delhi in an randomised trial that found large gains in mathematics and Hindi (Muralidharan et al., 2019). The software adjusts the difficulty of each question to the child's level, which is especially useful in classrooms where  children in the same grade may be several years apart in what they know.

The second group covers smartphone apps that families use at home. These range from simple reading apps that show a story with audio narration to full courses that follow the state syllabus. Evidence on these tools are thinner, partly because usage at home is harder to measure and partly because many families share one phone between several children.

The third group covers tools for teachers rather than students, such as video lessons on teaching methods or apps that help teachers group children by level. These are included because they are often delivered in Indian languages and can change what happens in the classroom even when children never touch a device.

## What does the evidence show?

### Adaptive software

The strongest evidence comes from adaptive software used in structured settings. Across six randomised trials, children who used such tools for at least two sessions an week gained between 0.2 and 0.6 standard deviations in mathematics over a school year. Gains in language were smaller but still positive. The effects were largest for children who started furthest behind, which suggests that these tools are particularly useful for remidial teaching.

Language of instruction appears to matter. In a trial in Rajasthan, children who used a Hindi version of a mathematics app gained more than children who used the same app in English, even though both groups were taught in Hindi at school (Sharma and Gupta, 2021). The authors argue that children spent less effort decoding instructions and more effort on the mathematics itself.

### Home-based apps

Results from home-based apps are mixed. Several studies found high initial use that faded within a few weeks. In one large study in Uttar Pradesh, more than 60% of families who downloaded a reading app stopped using it within a month. Those who continued were mostly from households where a parent had finished secondary school, which raises the risk that such apps widen rather than close learning gaps.

Some programmes have tried to keep families engaged by sending reminders through WhatsApp or by asking teachers to check progress. These measures helped, but there effects were modest. A study in Karnataka found that weekly calls from a volunteer rose the share of children still using an app after three months from 18% to 31%.

### Teacher-facing tools

Evidence on teacher-facing tools is the most limited. Teachers generally valued video lessons in their own language and reported using ideas from them, but few studies measured whether children learned more as a result. One exception is an evaluation in Telangana, where teachers who received short videos on teaching at the right level, together with monthly coaching visits, raised their students' reading scores by 0.15 standard deviations (Rao et al., 2022).

## Cost and scale

Costs vary widely. Adaptive software in computer labs is the most expensive per child, mostly because of hardware and staff. Estimates range from ₹1,500 to ₹4,000 per child per year. Home-based apps are cheaper to deliver once built, often under ₹200 per child, but building good content in many languages is costly. Translating an app is not enough; examples, names and stories must also be adapted so that children recognise their own world in thme.

Several states have tried to bring vernacular EdTech into government schools at scale. Andhra Pradesh, Assam and Uttar Pradesh have all signed agreements with private providers. Little independent evidence exists on how these large programmes perform, and the few available reports suggest that usage is often far lower than planned because of device shortages, poor connectivity and limited teacher training.

## Gaps in the evidence

Three gaps stand  out. First, almost all rigorous studies are in Hindi, Kannada, Telugu or Marathi. We found no randomised evaluations of tools in Odia, Santali, Bodo or most other languages spoken by tribal communities, even though children in these communities face some of the largest learning gaps. Second, most studies follow follow children for a year or less, so we know little about whether gains last. Third, very few studies report costs in enough detail to compare tools fairly.

## Conclusion

Digital learning tools in Indian languages can raise learning, especially when they adapt to each child's level and are used regularly in a structured setting. Home-based apps are cheaper but harder to sustain, and they risk helping the children who need help least. The field would benefit from more studies in under-served languages, longer follow-ups and better cost data. Above all, the evidence suggests that the language of a tool is not a detail but a core part of it's design.
