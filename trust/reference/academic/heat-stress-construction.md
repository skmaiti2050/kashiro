# Heat Stress Among Construction Workers in Ahmedabad: A Mixed-Methods Study

## Abstract

Construction workers in Indian cities spend long hours outdoors during some of the hottest summers on record. This study combines wearable temperature sensors, daily health diaries and interviews to describe how heat affects 146 workers on 9 building sites in Ahmedabad between 1 April 2024 and 15 June 2024. We find that the wet-bulb globe temperature on site exceeded the recommended limit for heavy work on 52 of 76 working days. Workers reported headaches, dizziness and cramps far more often on these days, and output fell by about 11%. Shifting the start of the working day by two hours reduced heat exposure substantially without lowering daily output. We discuss how the Ahmedabad Heat Action Plan could be extended to private construction sites.

## 1. Introduction

India recorded its hottest April on record in 2024, and several cities in Gujarat and Rajasthan crossed 45 °C in May. Heat waves are no longer rare events but a regular part of the summer, and they fall hardest on people who work outdoors. Construction is among the largest employer of such workers. Most are migrants from rural districts, are paid by the day and have little ability to stop work when conditions become dangerous.

Ahmedabad was the first city in South Asia to adopt a Heat Action Plan, in 2013, after a heat wave in 2010 was linked to more than 1,300 excess deaths (Knowlton et al., 2014). The plan includes early warnings, cool roofs and extended hours at public health centres. However, its guidance for employers is advisory, and there is little evidence on how far private construction sites follow it.

This study set out to measure heat exposure directly on building sites and to understand how workers and contractors respond to it. The fieldwork was designed by Dr. Farhan Qureshi and carried out with the support of a local workers' union. Prof. Leela Desai advised on the physiological measures.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Sites and participants

We recruited nine sites through the union and two large contractors. Sites ranged from a four-storey residential block to a 22-storey commercial tower. All workers on each site were invited to take part, and 146 agreed. Most were men aged aged 18 to 40, though 23 women working as helpers and carriers also joined. Nearly all had migrated from Dahod, Panchmahal or neighbouring districts of Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan.

### 2.2 Measurements

Each site had a heat stress monitor that recorded wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) every 10 minutes. A subset of 48 workers also wore a small sensor that measured skin temperature and heart rate. At the end of each day, a field assistant asked every participant weather they had experienced headache, dizziness, cramps, nausea or extreme tiredness. Contractors provided daily records of work completed, such as the area of plaster applied or the number of blocks laid.

### 2.3 Interviews

We conducted 34 in-depth interviews with workers, supervisors and contractors. Interviews were held in Gujarati or Hindi and and lasted about an hour, recorded with consent and transcribed. We used a thematic approach to identify how people described heat, what they did to cope and what stopped them from taking breaks.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Exposure

The recommended WBGT limit for continuous heavy work is 28 °C (ISO 7243:2017). On 52 of the 76 working days in our study, the site average exceeded this limit for at least three hours. Peak values above 33 °C were recorded on 19 days, mostly in May. Conditions were worst on upper floors without shade and on sites where concrete was poured in the afternoon.

The wearable sensors told a consistent story. Among the 48 workers who wore them, average heart rate during the afternoon session was 14 beats per minute higher on hot days than on cooler ones, even though the work itself did not change. Skin temperature rose steadily after lunch and rarely returned to its morning level before the end of the shift. Workers who carried loads up stairs, such as bricks, cement bags or buckets of mortar, showed the highest readings. Several of them spent more than half the afternoon above the heart-rate threshold that occupational guidelines treat as a warning sign. These readings matter because they show strain building up over the day, not just at the hottest moment.

### 3.2 Symptoms and output

On days above the limit, workers were 2.6 times as likely to report at least one heat-related symptom. Headaches and extreme tiredness was the most common. Four workers fainted on site during the study period, and two of them needed treatment at a hospital.

Output fell on hot hot days. Comparing the same tasks on hot and cooler days, the area of plaster applied per worker dropped by about 11%. Contractors were aware of this loss, but most of them saw it as unavoidable. One supervisor said that "the work has a deadline, and the deadline does not care about teh weather."

### 3.3 The schedule change

On three sites, contractors agreed to start work at 6 a.m. instead of 8 a.m. for four weeks in May, with a long break from 12:30 p.m. to 3:30 p.m. and a shorter evening session. Heat exposure during working hours fell sharply, with time above the limit dropping from 4.1 hours to 1.3 hours per day. Workers also got a extra ten-minute pause before the evening session. Daily output did not fall; it rose slightly, probably because fewer workers stoped early or fell ill.

Workers welcomed teh change, though some found it difficult to rest in their temporary housing during the afternoon, which was often a tin-roofed room that was hotter then the site itself.

### 3.4 Barriers to rest

Interviews revealed several reasons why workers rarely took breaks even when they felt unwell. Most were paid by the day, and some feared being replaced if they were seen resting. Drinking water was available on all sites, but cool water and shaded rest areas were not. Women workers faced an additional problem: several sites had no toilets for women, so they drank less water to avoid needing one.

## 4. Discussion

Our results show that heat exposure on construction sites in Ahmedabad routinely exceeds safe limits, and that this has costs for both workers and employers. The schedule change on three sites suggests that a simple, low-cost measure can reduce exposure without hurting productivity. This is consistent with studies from Qatar and Central America that found similar benefits from shifting working hours (Flouris et al., 2018).

Policy has has not yet caught up. The Heat Action Plan encourages employers to adjust working hours, but compliance is voluntary and its unclear who would enforce it on private sites. Building and Other Construction Workers welfare boards, which collect a cess on construction projects, could fund shaded rest areas, cool drinking water and toilets for women. In Gujarat, the board had an unspent balance of more than ₹3,000 crore in 2023, according to figures presented in the state assembly.

The study has limitations. Our sample is small and drawn from sites where contractors agreed to take part, who may be more careful than average. Symptoms were self-reported, and workers may have under-reported them to avoid seeming weak. We also could not follow workers after they left a site, so we do not know about longer-term effects such as kidney damage, which has been reported among outdoor workers in other hot countries.

## 5. Conclusion

Heat is already a daily occupational hazard on Ahmedabad's construction sites, and it will become more severe as summers grow hotter. Starting work earlier and resting through the hottest hours reduced exposure by more than two thirds without any loss of output. Turning this from a voluntary practice into a standard requirement, backed by funding for rest areas and water, would protect a workforce that has few power to protect itself.
