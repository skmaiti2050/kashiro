# Small Town, Big Plans: Episode 12

**Recording script and show notes. Guest: Ms. Nandini Joshi, founder of a dairy cooperative app in Kolhapur. Host: Arjun.**

---

**[INTRO MUSIC FADES]**

**ARJUN:** Hello and welcome back to Small Town, Big Plans, the podcast about people building companies far away from the startup hubs. I'm your host, Arjun. Today's guest runs a business that most of you will never have heard of, but if you live in western Maharashtra, there's a good chance your morning milk passed through her software. She's the founder of a company that helps dairy cooperatives track milk collection, quality and payments. Nandini, welcome to the show.

**NANDINI:** Thank you, Arjun. I've been listening for a while, so it's nice to be on the other side of the microphone.

**ARJUN:** Let's start at teh beginning. You grew up in Kolhapur, right?

**NANDINI:** Yes, in a village about twenty kilometres outside the city. My father was a farmer and also the secretary of our village milk society, so I grew up around milk collection. Every morning and every evening, farmers would bring their cans to the collection centre, and my father would test the fat content, write everything in a big register, and calculate how much each farmer should be paid. It was slow, and mistakes happen all the time.

**ARJUN:** And that's where the idea came from?

**NANDINI:** Not directly. I went to Pune for engineering, then worked for four years at an IT company in Hinjewadi. I was writing software for a bank, which was fine, but I didn't feel connected to it. Then in 2019, my father fell ill, and I came home for a few months to help. I ended up running the collection centre myself. And I realised how much time was being wasted, and how often farmers was being paid late or paid the wrong amount.

**ARJUN:** What did you do?

**NANDINI:** At first, I just built an spreadsheet. Then a simple app on my phone that let me enter the readings from the fat-testing machine and calculate payments automatically. Farmers started asking for receipts on WhatsApp, so I added that. Within a few months, two neighbouring societies asked if they could use it too.

**ARJUN:** So the customers came to you.

**NANDINI:** They did. I didn't plan to start a company. But by the end of 2020, we had eleven societies using the app, and I had to decide whether to go back to Pune or do do this full time.

**[SHORT MUSIC STING]**

**ARJUN:** Let's talk about that decision. A lot of our listeners are in exactly that position: they have a stable job in a big city, and an idea that only makes sense back home. How did you decide?

**NANDINI:** Honestly, it was was terrifying. My salary in Pune was good, and my mother was worried. People in the village thought I had failed in the city and come back. But I sat down and did the numbers. If I could get 100 societies paying ₹1,500 a month each, I'd be earning more than my old salary, and I'd be doing something that actually helped people I knew.

**ARJUN:** And did you get to a hundred?

**NANDINI:** We crossed a hundred in March 2022. Today we have about 640 societies across Kolhapur, Sangli and Satara, and we're starting in parts of Karnataka. We process payments for roughly 85,000 farmers.

**ARJUN:** That's remarkable. How did you grow without the kind of funding that city startups get?

**NANDINI:** Slowly, and mostly through word of of mouth. Milk societies talk to each other. When one secretary sees that another society is paying farmers on time every ten days without mistakes, they want the same thing. We also worked with the district cooperative union, which helped a lot. Their chairman, Mr. Shivaji Patil, was one of our early supporters. He invited us to speak at their annual meeting, and we signed thirty societies in a single week after that.

**ARJUN:** What about money? Did you raise funding?

**NANDINI:** We took a small grant from a state government programme in 2021, about ₹10 lakh, and later a seed round from an angel network in Pune. But for the first two years, we ran on revenue. Our costs are low. Most of are team is from the area, and we work out of a rented office above a bank branch in Kolhapur.

**ARJUN:** I want to ask about the technology, because I think people imagine rural tech as something very basic.

**NANDINI:** It's not basic, but it has to be simple to use. That's different. Our app has to work on cheap Android phones with patchy internet. It has to work in Marathi and Kannada. It has to connect to fat-testing machines from five different manufacturers, each with it's own data format. And it has to be fast, because at six in the morning there are fifty farmers in a queue and nobody wants to wait.

**ARJUN:** What was the hardest technical problem?

**NANDINI:** Offline sync, without an doubt. Many collection centres lose their internet connection for hours. The app has to keep working, store everything locally, and then sync when the connection comes back, without ever losing or duplicating a record. Getting that right took us almost a year, and we still find edge cases.

**[SHORT MUSIC STING]**

**ARJUN:** Let's talk about the farmers. What has changed for them?

**NANDINI:** The biggest change are trust. Before, a farmer had no way of checking whether the fat reading or the payment was correct. Now, they get a WhatsApp message within a minute of pouring their milk, with the quantity, the fat and SNF readings, and the rate. At the end of each payment cycle, they get a statement. Disputes have dropped dramatically.

The second change is speed. Many societies used to pay once a month, and sometimes late. Now most of them pay every ten days, directly into the farmer's bank account. For a small farmer with two or three buffaloes, getting paid faster makes a real difference.

**ARJUN:** Have there been any surprises?

**NANDINI:** Women farmers. In many households, women do most of the work with the animals, but the money was paid to the men. Several societies now let farmers choose whose account the payment goes to, and we've seen a big shift. In some villages, more then forty per cent of payments now go to women.

**ARJUN:** That's a powerful outcome.

**NANDINI:** It is. And we didnt design for it. We just made it easy to change the account, and people did the rest.

**ARJUN:** Before we wrap up, what advice would you give to someone who wants to build a company in a small town?

**NANDINI:** Three things. First, solve a problem you have seen with your own eyes. Don't build something because it worked in Bengaluru. Second, be patient. Trust takes time in small communities, but once you have it, it spreads quickly. And third, hire locally. The people who understand your customers best are often already living next door to them.

**ARJUN:** Nandini Joshi, thank you so much for joining us.

**NANDINI:** Thank you, Arjun. This was fun.

**[OUTRO MUSIC]**

**ARJUN:** That's it for this episode of Small Town, Big Plans. If you enjoyed it, please share it with a friend who's thinking about going home to build something. Next week, we're talking to a team in Siliguri that makes tea-processing equipment. Until then, take care.
