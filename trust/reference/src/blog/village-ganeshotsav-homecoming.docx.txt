# Going Home for Ganeshotsav: Notes from My Village in Ratnagiri

Every year, around the end of August, half of Mumbai seems to empty out towards the Konkan. The trains are booked months in advance, the ST buses run extra services, and the highway is a slow river of cars with roof carriers full of bags, sweets and decorations. I am one of those people. My family's village is near Sangameshwar in Ratnagiri district, and for as long as I can remember, we have gone home for Ganeshotsav. This year I finally wrote down what the ten days look like, mostly so that my nephew, who was born in Pune, has something to read when he is older.

## The journey

We left Thane at [[4:15 a.m.]] on [[26 August 2025]], hoping to cross Panvel before the traffic built up. We did not. By the time we reached the ghat section near Kashedi, it was raining so hard that the wipers could barely keep up, and a truck had broken down on one of the hairpin bends. We waited for nearly two hours, eating the batata vadas my aunt had packed and listening to my father explain, for {{teh|the|s}} twentieth year in a row, that the old road through Mahad was better.

We reached the village a little after four in the afternoon. The last stretch, from the highway to our house, is a narrow road between paddy fields, and in August the fields are so green that they almost glow. My grandmother was waiting on the verandah, as she does every year, and the first thing she asked was whether we had eaten. We had, but it made no difference. Within ten minutes there was hot tea, poha and a plate of ukadiche modak in front of us.

## Preparing for Bappa

Our family brings home a clay Ganpati idol on the first day of the festival. My cousin Ajinkya {{collect|collects|j}} it from the murtikar in the next village, who has been making idols for our family for three generations. The idol is about two feet tall, painted in soft colours, and it arrives covered with {{an|a|s}} cloth so that nobody sees its face before the pranpratishtha.

The day before, the whole house is busy. The women of the family clean the puja room, the children make paper decorations, and the men argue about the lighting. This year my uncle insisted on fairy lights in the shape of a peacock, which took four people and most of the evening to put up. The village electrician, Santosh, came twice to fix the wiring and refused to take more than [[₹200]], saying that it was Bappa's work.

## The ten days

Ganeshotsav in the village is very different from the festival in the city. There are no huge pandals or loudspeakers playing film songs all night. Instead, every house has its own Ganpati, and people walk from house to house in the evening for aarti and darshan. By the end of the first night, I had sung the same aarti in eleven houses and eaten far too many modaks.

The days have{{  | |s}}a slow rhythm. In the morning, after the puja, the men go to the fields or to the bazaar in Sangameshwar, and the women start cooking for the afternoon naivedya. Lunch is always a long affair: rice, varan, usal, solkadhi, and at least two kinds of sweets. In the evening, the bhajan mandal from the temple goes from house to house with dholki and tal, and the singing can go on until midnight.

On the fifth day, we had a small gathering for the Gauri puja, when the women of the family bring home the goddess Gauri and keep her with Ganpati for three days. My aunt, who is the most organised person I know, made a list of {{everything everything|everything|s}} that was needed and stuck it on the kitchen door. It included forty items, from the saree for the goddess to the exact number of bhakris to make for the dinner.

## Money and changes

People in the city sometimes ask me how much the festival costs. It is hard to say, because so much of it is shared. Our family spends about [[₹35,000]] in total, including the idol, decorations, food for guests and gifts for the people who help. The idol itself costs [[₹4,500]] this year, up from [[₹3,800]] last year, which the murtikar blamed on the rising price of shadu clay and paint.

The village has changed in other ways too. Most families now have relatives in Mumbai, Pune or the Gulf, and many houses stay locked for most of the year. The school has fewer children every year. Several of the old tiled houses have been replaced by concrete ones, and the paddy fields closest to the road are slowly turning into plots for holiday homes. My grandmother says the festival is the only time the village feels full again.

There are good changes as well. The road is finally tarred, the gram panchayat has built a new water tank, and the anganwadi now has a proper building with a toilet. Almost every house has internet, which means that relatives who cannot travel join the aarti on video calls. This year my uncle in Dubai attended every evening aarti on his phone, and the children took turns holding it up to the idol so he could see.

## Visarjan

On the last day, the whole village walks together to the river for the visarjan. The idols are carried on shoulders or on small handcarts, and the procession moves slowly, with dhol-tasha, gulal and a lot of shouting of "Ganpati Bappa Morya, pudhchya varshi lavkar ya!" It means "Come back soon next year", and it is the line {{everybody|everybody's|j}} remembers.

The river was high this year after the heavy rain, so the gram panchayat had marked a safe spot with ropes and posted volunteers to help. Some families have started using artificial tanks or eco-friendly idols that dissolve quickly, after complaints that paint and plaster were damaging the river. Our idol is made of shadu clay, which dissolves within a few hours, and we use natural colours, but I know {{its|it's|j}} not the case everywhere.

When the idols are gone, the village suddenly feels empty. People stand around for a while on the riverbank, talking quietly, and then walk home in small groups. That evening the house is strangely silent. My grandmother always says the same thing on that night: "Bappa has gone, now we also have to go."

## Going back

We left the next morning, with the car full of {{mangoes pickle|mango pickle|j}}, kokum syrup, rice from the fields and a bag of modaks for the neighbours in Thane. The traffic on the way back was worse than on the way there, and it took us almost eleven hours. Nobody complained much. There is a kind of tiredness after the festival that feels more like contentment {{then|than|j}} fatigue.

If you have never been to a village Ganeshotsav, I would encourage you to go, especially in the Konkan. It is not glamorous, and you will probably be asked to help peel coconuts or carry chairs. But you will see the festival as it has been celebrated for {{a generations|generations|j}}: slow, shared and full of food.
