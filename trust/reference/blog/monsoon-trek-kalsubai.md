# Chasing Clouds: A Monsoon Trek to Kalsubai

I have climbed Kalsubai three times now, and every time I promise myself that the next trip will be in winter, when the trail is dry and the views go on forever. And every time, the first heavy rain of July arrives and I find myself packing a raincoat again. There is something about the Sahyadris in the monsoon that pulls you back, even when you know you will come home soaked to the bone with mud up to your knees.

This post covers my most recent trip, on 20 July 2025, with four friends from Pune. If you are planning your first monsoon trek, I hope it helps you prepare. If you have done Kalsubai before, I hope it makes you want to go back.

## Getting there

Kalsubai is the highest peak in Maharashtra, at about 1,646 metres. The usual starting point is Bari, a small village near Bhandardara, which is roughly four hours by road from Pune and three from Mumbai. We left left Pune at 3:30 a.m. in a rented Innova, which cost us ₹6,500 for the day including fuel and tolls. Splitting that five ways made it cheaper than the bus, and a lot more comfortable.

The road after Igatpuri are narrow and full of potholes in the rain, so drive slowly. We stopped for chai and vada pav at a tiny stall just before the turn to Bari, and I would recommend you to do the same. It was the last hot food we had until lunch, and the old man who ran the stall gave us useful advice about the trail conditions.

## The trail

The climb is about 6 km each way, with an height gain of roughly 900 metres. In dry weather a fit person can reach the top in three hours. In the monsoon, plan for four or more. The first section passes through paddy fields that were bright green when we went, with farmers already planting rice in teh flooded terraces. After that the trail climbs steeply through patches of forest and open grassland.

There are several iron ladders on the steepest sections, put up by the forest department and local villagers. They are sturdy, but the rungs get slippery, so take your time and keep three points of contact. On our trip, one of the ladders had a loose railing, and a local guide was standing there to help people across. We tipped him ₹100 and he seemed genuinely surprised, which made me think that that most trekkers don't bother.

About halfway up, the clouds rolled in and we lost all visibility for nearly an hour. We could hear other groups ahead of us and behind us, but we could not see more than ten metres in any direction. This is normal in July, and the trail is well marked with painted arrows, but it is easy to take a wrong turn if you are not paying attention. Stay close to  your group.

## At the top

The summit has a small temple to Kalsubai Devi, and on weekends it gets crowded. When we arrived at around 10:15 a.m., there were at least a hundred people at the top, many of them college students who had camped overnight in the village. The view was mostly cloud, but every few minutes the wind would open a gap and we could see the Bhandardara reservoir far below, shining silver in the light. Those few seconds were worth the entire climb.

We ate our packed lunch, sandwiches with pickle that my friend Snehal's mother had made for us, and rested for about forty minutes before starting down. Do not stay too long at the top in the monsoon. The weather can change quickly, and the descent is harder then the climb.

## Coming down

The descent took us almost as long as the climb. The mud on the lower sections had turned into something closer to soup, and all five of us fell at least once. My friend Aditya fell three times and decided, very loudly, that he was never coming back. He has already asked me when we are going next.

Good shoes make an huge difference here. I wore trekking shoes with deep lugs, which gripped well even in thick mud. Two of my friends wore running shoes, and they spent most of the descent sliding. If you do not want to buy trekking shoes, the rubber sandals that local villagers wear are surprisingly good, and you can buy them in Bari for about ₹300.

## Staying overnight

If you would rather not do the whole trip in one long day, several families in Bari run simple guesthouses. You get a mattress on the floor, a shared bathroom and a home-cooked dinner of rice and lentils with vegetables, usually for around ₹800 per person. Staying the night lets you start the climb at first light, before the crowds arrive, and you will have the summit almost to yourself if the weather is kind. The family we spoke to said that weekends in July are booked weeks in advance, so call ahead. Weekdays are much quieter and the hosts have more time to chat.

## What to carry

Here is what I packed packed, and what I wish I had packed:

- A good raincoat or poncho. An umbrella is useless on the ladders.
- A small backpack with a rain cover, and everything inside in plastic bags.
- Two litres of water. There are a few stalls on the trail selling lemon juice and buttermilk, but don't rely on then.
- Snacks with salt, like chikki, peanuts and biscuits.
- A spare pair of socks and a dry T-shirt in a sealed bag. Changing into dry clothes at the bottom felt like a luxury.
- A small first-aid kit with plasters, an elastic bandage and some painkillers.
- Cash. There is no network for most of the trail, and the stalls don't take UPI when the signal is down.

What I wish I had carried: a pair of gloves for the ladders, and a towel for the car ride home.

## Safety

Every monsoon there are stories of trekkers getting lost or injured in the Sahyadris, and some of these accidents are serious. Please check the weather forecast before you go, and do not attempt the trek if heavy rain or a red alert has been announced. Tell someone at home where you are going and when you expect to be back. If you are new to trekking, go with an experienced group or hire a local guide in Bari; the guides charge around ₹1,000 for a group and know the trail far better than any app.

Also, please carry your rubbish back down. The trail was much cleaner this year than on my previous trips, thanks to clean-up drives by local groups, but I still saw plastic bottles and chip packets thrown into the bushes. The mountain deserve better.

## Was it worth it?

Absolutely. By the time we reached the car, we were wet, muddy, sore and very happy. We stopped at a dhaba near Igatpuri on the way back and ate the best misal of our lives, though I suspect anything would have tasted good by then. We were back in Pune by 9 p.m., exhausted but already talking about the next trip.

If you have been thinking about a monsoon trek, Kalsubai is a great choice. Its challenging enough to feel like an achievement, but not so hard that a reasonably fit beginner can't manage it with care. Just go prepared, respect the weather and don't forget your spare socks.
