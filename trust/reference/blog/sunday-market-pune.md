# Sunday Mornings at the Old Market

Most Sundays, before the city wakes up properly, I walk to the old market near my parents' house in Pune. I have been doing this since I was a child, first holding my grandfather's hand and later on my own, with a cloth bag and a list that I never quite follow. The market has changed a lot in thirty years, but the feeling of walking in at seven in the morning, when the vegetables are still wet and the vendors are still arranging their piles, has not changed at all.

This post is a love letter to that market, and to the people who keep it running.

## Getting there early

The market opens at around around six, but the real activity starts closer to seven. By nine, it is crowded with families, and by eleven many of the best stalls have sold out. If you want the freshest vegetables and the most patient vendors, go early.

I usually park my scooter on the lane behind the temple, where a man named Ganpat has been collecting parking money for as long as I can remember. He charges ₹10, gives you a paper slip, and somehow remembers every vehicle without ever looking at the slips again.

## The vegetable lanes

The heart of the the market is three long lanes of vegetable sellers. Each vendor sits on a raised platform with their produce arranged in neat pyramids: tomatoes, onions, potatoes, brinjals, bottle gourds and whatever leafy greens is in season. In winter there are carrots so red they look painted, and fresh green peas that my mother still shells by hand on the balcony.

My favourite vendor is Sushila Tai, who has had the same spot near the entrance for more than twenty years. She does not use a digital scale. She weighs everything on an old brass balance with iron weights, and she always adds a handful of coriander and a couple of green chillies for free. When I tried to pay her by UPI for the first time, she laughed and pointed to a QR code taped to the side of her basket. Her grandson had set it up, she said, and now half her customers pay that way.

Prices varies through the year. Last Sunday, on 12 October 2025, tomatoes were ₹40 a kilo, onions ₹35 and potatoes ₹30. A bunch of fenugreek was ₹15. These numbers are higher than when I started coming, of course, but still well below what the supermarkets charge.

## The fruit corner

At the far end of the vegetable lanes is the fruit corner, which changes completely with the seasons. In summer it smells of mangoes, and the vendors argue loudly about whether the Alphonso from Ratnagiri is better than the one from Devgad. In the monsoon there are jamuns that stain your fingers purple and small, sour plums that I have never seen anywhere else. In winter there are guavas, oranges and strawberries from Mahabaleshwar.

The strawberry seller, a young man called Nikhil, told me that he drives to Mahabaleshwar at two in the morning every Saturday to buy directly from the farms. He sells out by ten. "If I come late, the hotels take everything," he said. He was charging ₹120 for a small box, and I bought two.

## Breakfast

No Sunday at the market is complete without breakfst. There is a small stall near the fruit corner that sells the best poha I have ever eaten, served in a paper plate with a slice of lemon and a sprinkle of sev on top. A plate costs ₹30, and the tea is ₹15. The owner, Mr. Kulkarni, has been running the stall since 1994. His son now handles the cooking, but Mr. Kulkarni still sits at the counter every morning, taking money and telling customers about the old days.

I usually eat standing at teh counter, watching the market fill up around me. It is the best people-watching spot in the city. You see grandparents with grandchildren, young couples arguing gently about which cabbage to buy, and restaurant owners loading huge sacks of onions into the back of their vans.

## The flower sellers

Just outside the main shed, along the wall of the temple, sit the flower sellers. They arrive even earlier than the vegetable vendors, sometimes before five, with baskets of marigolds, roses and jasmine bought at the wholesale market the night before. By the time I arrive, the air there smells sweet and heavy, and the women are threading flowers into long strings with a speed that I still find hard to follow.

Most of their customers buy flowers for the morning prayer at home, but on festival days the demand is enormous. Before Dussehra and Diwali, the flower sellers spill out onto the road, and the prices of marigold strings can double overnight. One of the sellers, an elderly woman who calls everyone "beta," once told me that she earns more in the week before Diwali than in the rest of the month. She has been sitting at the same spot for over over forty years, and she remembers my grandfather well.

## What has changed

The market is not what it was when I was a child. Several of the old stalls have closed, and some of the vendors I grew up with have retired or passed away. The municipal corporation rebuilt the main shed in 2018, which made it cleaner and safer but also removed some of its character. Many families now order their vegetables through apps, and on rainy Sundays the lanes can feel emptier.

But some things have changed for the better. There are proper drains now,  so the lanes no longer flood in the monsoon. Most vendors accept digital payments, which means I no longer have to hunt for change. And a group of local volunteers runs a cloth bag stall at the entrance, where you can borrow a bag and return it the next week. Plastic bags has almost disappeared from the market.

## Why I keep going

I could buy vegetables online. It would be faster and probably more convenient. But I would miss the conversations, the smells and the small acts of kindness that make the market what it is. I would miss Sushila Tai's free coriander and Mr. Kulkarni's stories. I would miss knowing exactly where my food comes from and who grew it.

The market is also one of the few places in the city where people from very different backgrounds meet as equals. The vendor and the customer bargain, joke and complain about the weather together, and for a few minutes nobody cares about anything else.

If you live in Pune, or is visiting, I encourage you to find your own neighbourhood market and go early on a Sunday. Take a cloth bag and some cash, and leave your list at home. Let teh market decide what you cook that week.
