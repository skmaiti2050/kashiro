# What I Learned Taking My First Home Loan

When my wife and I decided to buy a flat in Hyderabad last year, we thought the hard part would be finding the right place. We were wrong. Finding the flat took three three months. Understanding the home loan took longer, and we are still learning. I am writing this post for anyone who is about to go through the same process, because I wish someone had explained these things to me in plain language before we signed anything.

A quick disclaimer first: I am not a financial adviser. I work in software, and everything here comes from our own experience and a lot of late-night reading. Please check the details with your bank and, if you can, with a professional before making decisions.

## The flat and the numbers

We bought a two-bedroom flat in Kondapur for ₹82,00,000, including registration and stamp duty. We had saved about ₹22,00,000 over six years, so we needed a loan of ₹60,00,000. Most banks will lend up to 80% of the property value for loans of this size, so on paper we were comfortably within the limit.

We applied to four lenders: two large public sector banks, one private bank and one housing finance company. The interest rates they quoted ranged from 8.40% to 9.15%, which doesn't sound like a big difference until you work out what it means over twenty years. On a loan of ₹60 lakh, the gap between those two rates is roughly ₹6 lakh in total interest. That was a eye-opener for us.

## Fixed, floating and the fine print

Almost all home loans in India today are floating-rate loans linked to an external benchmark, usually the Reserve Bank of India's repo rate. When the repo rate goes up, your interest rate goes up too, and when it falls, your rate should fall. I say "should" because we found that banks are much faster at passing on increases then decreases.

The rate you pay is the benchmark plus a spread. The spread is fixed for the life of the loan unless you renegotiate it, and this is where your credit score matters. With a score above 780, we were offered a spread that was nearly half a percentage point lower than what a friend with a score of 710 got from the same bank. If you are planning to borrow in the next year or two, look after you're credit score now. Pay every bill on time and keep your credit card balance low.

## EMI versus tenure

When rates rose in 2022 and 2023, many borrowers discovered that their banks had kept the EMI the same and quietly increased the tenure instead. Some people's twenty-year loans became thirty-year loans without them noticing. The Reserve Bank of India issued guidelines in August 2023 asking banks to inform borrowers clearly and give them a choice, but you should still check your loan statement every few months.

We chose a tenure of 20 years, which gave us an EMI of about ₹52,000 a month at our rate of 8.50%. That is a little over a third of our combined take-home pay. It feels tight, but manageable, and we plan to prepay whenever we recieve a bonus.

## Prepayment is your friend

This was the single most useful thing we learned. On a floating-rate home loan taken by an individual, banks cannot charge a penalty for prepayment. Every extra rupee you pay goes straight to reducing the principal, and because interest is calculated on the outstanding principal, even small prepayments in the early years save a surprising amount.

I built an simple spreadsheet to test this. Prepaying just ₹1,00,000 a year, on top of the regular EMI, would shorten our loan by about six years and save us more than ₹18 lakh in interest. Seeing those numbers changed how we think about our bonuses and tax refunds. They are not spending money anymore; they goes into the loan.

## Tax benefits

Home loans come with tax benefits, but they depend on which tax regime you choose. Under the old regime, you can claim a deduction of up to ₹1,50,000 on principal repayment under Section 80C, and up to ₹2,00,000 on interest under Section 24(b) for a self-occupied property. Under the new regime, which is now the default, these deductions are not available for a self-occupied home.

We sat down with a chartered accountant, Mr. Venkat Raman, who walked us through both options with our actual numbers. For us, the old regime still came out slightly ahead because of the home loan interest, but the gap was smaller than I expected. Your situation may be different, so do the calculation rather than assuming.

## The paperwork

Be ready for a a lot of paperwork. Every lender asked for salary slips, bank statements, Form 16, PAN and Aadhaar for both of us, and then a long list of property documents. For an under-construction flat, the bank also needs the builder's approvals and a tripartite agreement between you, the builder and the bank.

The legal and technical checks  took about three weeks. During this time the bank sent a valuer to the site and a lawyer reviewed the title documents. One bank rejected our application at this stage because the builder had not yet received an occupancy certificate for an earlier tower in the same project. It was frustrating, but in hindsight I'm glad someone was checking.

## Hidden costs

Budget for costs beyond the down payment. We paid a processing fee of 0.35% of the loan amount, plus GST, which came to about ₹24,780. There were also legal and valuation charges, a fee for registering the mortgage, and the cost of home insurance, which our bank strongly encouraged us to buy from its partner. You are not not required to buy insurance from the bank's partner, by the way. We compared a few policies and found one that was cheaper and had better cover.

Then there is the flat itself. Furniture, kitchen fittings, curtains, lights and a hundred small things added up to nearly ₹4,00,000 in the first two months. Nobody warned us about that.

## What I would do differently

If I could go back, I would start comparing lenders earlier and negotiate harder on the spread. I would also read the sanction letter line by line before signing, instead of skimming it in the bank branch while the relationship manager waited. And I would set up an automatic monthly prepayment from the very first month, even if it was small.

Buying a home is one of the biggest financial decisions most of us make. Its worth taking the time to understand the loan that comes with it. If you have questions, leave them in the comments and I'll do my best to answer, or at least point you to someone who knows more than I do.
